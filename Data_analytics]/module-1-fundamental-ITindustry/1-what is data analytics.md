# What Is Data Analytics?

Data analytics is the process of collecting, cleaning, organizing, and examining data to find useful patterns, answer questions, support decisions, and communicate findings. It can use statistics, visualization, and computer tools.

For example, a shop can analyze its sales data to discover which products sell best, when demand is highest, and where stock needs to be replenished.

## Common Steps in Data Analytics

1. **Define the question** — Decide what you need to learn.
2. **Collect data** — Gather relevant information from suitable sources.
3. **Clean and prepare data** — Correct errors, handle missing values, and organize it for analysis.
4. **Analyze data** — Use comparisons, calculations, statistical methods, or models to find patterns.
5. **Interpret and communicate results** — Explain what the findings mean, often with charts or reports.
6. **Take action and review** — Use the findings to make a decision, then check the outcome.

## Types of Data

There is no single list of data types that applies to every purpose. Data can be classified in several ways, and one dataset can belong to multiple categories at once.

### 1. By Structure

- **Structured data** — Organized in a fixed format, usually rows and columns. It is easy to search and analyze with databases or spreadsheets.  
  *Examples: customer tables, transaction records, inventory lists.*
- **Semi-structured data** — Has some organization or labels, but not a strict tabular structure.  
  *Examples: JSON, XML, emails, and many application logs.*
- **Unstructured data** — Does not follow a predefined table-like format.  
  *Examples: documents, social media posts, photographs, audio, and video.*

### 2. By Whether It Is Categorical or Numerical

- **Qualitative (categorical) data** — Describes a group, quality, or label rather than an amount.
  - **Nominal** — Categories with no natural order. *Examples: eye colour, country, product type.*
  - **Ordinal** — Categories with a meaningful order, but the gaps between categories are not necessarily equal. *Examples: small/medium/large, satisfaction ratings.*
- **Quantitative (numerical) data** — Represents a count or measured amount.
  - **Discrete** — Countable values, commonly whole numbers. *Examples: number of orders, number of website visits.*
  - **Continuous** — Measured values that can take any value within a range. *Examples: height, temperature, elapsed time.*

### 3. By Measurement Scale

- **Nominal** — Labels only; there is no ranking. *Example: payment method.*
- **Ordinal** — Ordered categories; distances between ranks are not known to be equal. *Example: a one-to-five-star rating.*
- **Interval** — Equal differences are meaningful, but zero does not mean “none.” *Example: temperature in Celsius.*
- **Ratio** — Equal differences are meaningful and zero represents none, so ratios are meaningful. *Examples: age, distance, sales revenue.*

### 4. Common Data Formats and Values

- **Text** — Words, sentences, or documents.
- **Boolean** — One of two values, such as true/false or yes/no.
- **Date and time** — Dates, times, or timestamps.
- **Geospatial** — Locations, coordinates, routes, or geographic shapes.
- **Image, audio, and video** — Media data, often unstructured.
- **Identifiers** — Values used to distinguish records, such as an order number. An identifier may contain digits but is usually a label, not a quantity to calculate.

### 5. By Source

- **Primary data** — Collected directly for the current purpose. *Examples: surveys, interviews, and experiments.*
- **Secondary data** — Previously collected by another person or organization and reused. *Examples: public statistics, research reports, and existing company records.*

### 6. By Time Arrangement

- **Cross-sectional data** — Observations from many subjects at one point or period in time. *Example: a survey of households this month.*
- **Time-series data** — Observations about a subject recorded over time. *Example: daily sales for a year.*
- **Panel (longitudinal) data** — Observations about the same subjects across multiple time periods. *Example: yearly income records for the same households.*

## Four Common Types of Analytics

These describe the questions an analysis answers, rather than types of data:

- **Descriptive analytics** — What happened?
- **Diagnostic analytics** — Why did it happen?
- **Predictive analytics** — What is likely to happen?
- **Prescriptive analytics** — What action should be taken?
