# What Is the SDLC?

**SDLC** stands for the **Software Development Life Cycle**. It is a structured process for planning, creating, testing, delivering, and maintaining software. It helps teams build software that meets user needs while managing time, cost, quality, security, and risk.

SDLC is used for many kinds of software, including websites, mobile apps, business systems, embedded software, and large enterprise applications. The exact phases and their order depend on the chosen development approach. In Agile, for example, teams revisit many activities in short iterations instead of completing each phase only once.

## Common SDLC Phases

### 1. Planning and Initial Investigation

- **What happens:** Define the problem, goals, scope, stakeholders, constraints, and high-level requirements.
- **Where it is useful:** At the start of a new product or project, or when deciding whether a proposed change is worth pursuing.
- **Important points:** Identify who will use the software, what success means, what is in and out of scope, and major risks. Clear scope helps prevent uncontrolled expansion.

### 2. Feasibility Analysis

- **What happens:** Assess whether the project is practical and worthwhile.
- **Where it is useful:** Before committing significant time or money, especially for large, costly, or technically uncertain projects.
- **Important points:** Consider technical feasibility, cost and expected value, timeline, staffing, legal or regulatory requirements, and operational impact. The result may be to proceed, change the proposal, or stop.

### 3. Requirements Analysis

- **What happens:** Gather, clarify, prioritize, and document what users and other stakeholders need.
- **Where it is useful:** Whenever a team needs a shared understanding of what the software must do and the constraints it must meet.
- **Important points:** Capture **functional requirements** (features and behavior) and **non-functional requirements** (such as performance, security, accessibility, reliability, and usability). Confirm assumptions with stakeholders and define acceptance criteria.

### 4. Design

- **What happens:** Plan how the software will meet its requirements, including its architecture, components, data, interfaces, and user experience.
- **Where it is useful:** Before implementation, and whenever a change has architectural, data, integration, or user-interface implications.
- **Important points:** Choose technologies that fit the requirements and constraints. Address security, privacy, accessibility, maintainability, scalability, and failure handling early. Design should be detailed enough to guide implementation without preventing useful feedback.

### 5. Implementation (Development or Coding)

- **What happens:** Developers build the software according to the design and requirements.
- **Where it is useful:** During feature development, fixes, and changes to existing software.
- **Important points:** Follow coding standards, use version control, review changes, manage dependencies, and test small parts as they are built. Secure coding and clear documentation reduce future defects and maintenance effort.

### 6. Testing and Verification

- **What happens:** Check that the software behaves as intended and meets its requirements; identify and fix defects.
- **Where it is useful:** Throughout development and before a release, with additional checks after changes.
- **Important points:** Testing can include unit, integration, system, acceptance, performance, security, accessibility, and usability testing. Test against requirements and realistic conditions. Testing reduces risk but cannot prove that software has no defects.

### 7. Deployment and Release

- **What happens:** Package and make the software available in its target environment, such as a production server, app store, or user device.
- **Where it is useful:** When delivering a new product, feature, or update to users.
- **Important points:** Plan configuration, data migration, backups, monitoring, access controls, and rollback or recovery. A staged release can limit impact if a problem occurs. Confirm that the deployed version works in its actual environment.

### 8. Operations and Maintenance

- **What happens:** Monitor, support, secure, and improve the software after release.
- **Where it is useful:** For software that is in use and needs to remain reliable, secure, compatible, and useful.
- **Important points:** Maintenance includes fixing defects, adapting to new environments, improving performance, and adding or refining features. Monitor service health, respond to incidents, apply security updates, back up important data, and plan ongoing support.

### 9. Retirement or Replacement

- **What happens:** Safely discontinue the software or replace it with another system.
- **Where it is useful:** When software is no longer needed, supported, secure, cost-effective, or suitable for its users.
- **Important points:** Communicate the change, migrate or archive data appropriately, manage access removal, meet retention obligations, and provide a transition plan. Retirement should avoid unexpected loss of service or information.

## Why the SDLC Is Useful

- **Creates a shared process** — Teams and stakeholders can understand what work is happening and what comes next.
- **Clarifies requirements and scope** — Helps reduce misunderstandings and unnecessary rework.
- **Improves quality** — Design reviews and testing find problems before or after release.
- **Manages risk** — Teams can identify technical, security, schedule, and business risks earlier.
- **Supports planning** — Estimating work, people, budget, and milestones is easier when activities are visible.
- **Makes maintenance manageable** — Documentation, monitoring, and planned support help software remain useful after launch.

## Common SDLC Development Models

These models organize or repeat SDLC work in different ways; they are not additional phases:

- **Waterfall** — Work proceeds through phases in a mostly sequential order; useful when requirements are stable and change is limited.
- **Agile** — Work is delivered in short iterations with regular feedback; useful when requirements may evolve and frequent releases are valuable.
- **Iterative or incremental** — Software is refined through repeated cycles or delivered in functional pieces; useful when learning and feedback should shape later versions.
- **Spiral** — Development repeats in cycles with explicit risk analysis; useful for large, complex, or high-risk projects.
- **DevOps-oriented delivery** — Development and operations collaborate using automation and continuous integration and delivery; useful when software needs frequent, reliable releases and ongoing operational feedback.

**In brief:** The SDLC provides a way to guide software from an initial need through delivery and ongoing support to eventual retirement. The right process depends on the project, its risks, its users, and how often requirements are expected to change.
